Abstract

--- PARA   6 [Normal] ---
This report presents an analytical queueing network model for the Google Online Boutique microservice benchmark, parameterised from 17,200 Jaeger telemetry spans across 1,247 traces (112 complete end-to-end and 1,135 partial/single-service traces). Eight service times are extracted from Jaeger spans; three (CartService, AdService, ShippingService) are estimated from instrumented code-path analysis due to a gRPC span-naming mismatch in the collector filter. The model identifies the Go Frontend as the sole binding bottleneck (D₀ = 80.00 ms, 80.00% CPU at λ₀ = 10 req/s, 43.2× larger demand than the second-heaviest service, 95.91% of total optimisation leverage). Empirical validation is available at one workload tier (λ₀ = 5.0 req/s), at which the pure-CPU model yields an Absolute Percentage Error of 51.47% (138.33 ms predicted vs. 285.0 ms observed), reflecting the omission of synchronous downstream blocking. Two independent measurements of Frontend CPU at λ=5 — 58% (cAdvisor 30-minute average) versus 2.58% (Prometheus 15-second rate) — diverge by 22× due to sampling-window semantics. All horizontal scaling and ROI projections are analytical model predictions.

1.0 Introduction: Why Microservice Performance is Hard

--- PARA  36 [Normal] ---
Modern cloud-native applications are constructed as loosely coupled microservices deployed on container orchestrators like Kubernetes. While this polyglot architecture improves team autonomy and continuous deployment velocity, it introduces severe performance engineering challenges.

--- PARA  37 [Normal] ---
The Performance Headache: Distributed Cascading Delays

--- PARA  38 [Normal] ---
In monolithic architectures, service communication occurs via in-process memory calls. In microservices, every user interaction triggers deep acyclic remote procedure call (RPC) trees over HTTP/REST and gRPC protocols. When a user checks out, upstream gateway services block synchronously while downstream dependencies (CartService, PaymentService, ShippingService) process requests. Minor tail-latency spikes or CPU contention in downstream services cascade upward, compounding into severe user-facing latency explosions.

--- PARA  39 [Normal] ---
The Testing Problem: Limitations of Physical Load Testing

--- PARA  40 [Normal] ---
Traditional performance engineering relies on physical load testing (e.g., Locust, k6, JMeter). In modern enterprise environments, physical load testing suffers from three critical flaws: it is slow (hours per configuration tier), expensive ($50–$200 per cloud run), and risky (cascading failures in staging environments).

--- PARA  41 [Normal] ---
This project demonstrates that mathematical Queueing Network (QN) and Layered Queueing Network (LQN) models provide instantaneous, cost-free performance forecasts. By parameterizing closed-form equations with distributed telemetry spans from Google Online Boutique [4] captured via Jaeger [5], Prometheus [6], and OpenTelemetry [7], engineers can evaluate scaling limits, size pod replicas, and prioritize software optimizations in sub-50 milliseconds. The model is validated at one empirical tier; all scaling results are analytical predictions.

2.2 The M/M/1 Queue (The Fundamental Building Block)

M/M/1 Residence Time [10]:
R_i = S_i / (1 − ρ_i)

--- PARA  48 [Normal] ---
•   = Mean residence / response time of microservice i (ms or seconds)

--- PARA  49 [Normal] ---
•   = Mean exclusive CPU service time in isolation (ms)

--- PARA  50 [Normal] ---
•   = Server utilisation fraction (λ_i × S_i)

--- PARA  51 [Normal] ---
•  = Server idle capacity headroom fraction (denominator driving non-linear explosion as ρ → 1.0)

--- PARA  52 [Normal] ---
Example: Frontend at 10 req/s: S_0 = 80.0 ms, ρ_0 = 0.800 ⟹ R_0 = 80.0 / (1 − 0.800) = 80.0 / 0.200 = 400.0 ms. At 5 req/s (ρ_0 = 0.400): R_0 = 80.0 / (1 − 0.400) = 133.3 ms (a 66.7% latency reduction for halving load).

Little's Law Queue Population [9]:
N_i = λ_i · R_i = ρ_i / (1 − ρ_i)

--- PARA  53 [Normal] ---
Example: Frontend at 10 req/s: N_0 = 10.0 req/s × 0.400 s = 4.0 concurrent requests (average active goroutine queue depth).

End-to-End System Response Time:
R_sys = Σ (V_i · R_i)

--- PARA  54 [Normal] ---
•   = End-to-end average system response time perceived by the external client (ms)

--- PARA  55 [Normal] ---
•   = Total number of monitored microservices in the application topology (K = 11)

--- PARA  56 [Normal] ---
•   = Visit ratio (mean number of invocations of microservice i per external user transaction)

--- PARA  57 [Normal] ---
•   = Mean residence time of microservice i (ms)

--- PARA  58 [Normal] ---
Example: At λ_0 = 10 req/s: R_sys = (1.000 × 400.0 ms) + (0.3692 × 5.09 ms) + ... + (0.0052 × 11.51 ms) = 405.02 ms.

2.3 Open Queueing Networks vs. Layered Queueing Networks (LQN)

--- PARA  60 [Normal] ---
A critical contribution of this study is establishing the distinction between Open Jackson Queueing Networks [8] and Layered Queueing Networks (LQN) [3] for cloud microservices. In an Open QN, microservices are assumed to process tasks independently. In reality, synchronous RPC calls cause parent threads to block while child services execute.

--- PARA  61 [Caption] ---
Table 2: Open Jackson QN vs. Layered Queueing Network (LQN) Architecture

--- PARA  62 [Normal] ---
Exclusive Service Demand Decomposition (LQN Demarcation):
S_i = D_(parent,i) − Σ_(j ∈ Children(i)) D_(child,j)

--- PARA  63 [Normal] ---
•   = Net exclusive CPU service demand of microservice i (ms)

--- PARA  64 [Normal] ---
•   = Gross execution span duration of service i recorded in distributed traces (ms)

--- PARA  65 [Normal] ---
•  Children(i) = Set of downstream microservices directly invoked by service i during request processing

--- PARA  66 [Normal] ---
•   = Measured gross response latency of downstream child service j (ms)

--- PARA  67 [Normal] ---
Example: Frontend gross span at 5 req/s = 285.0 ms; total synchronous child waiting time across 10 backends = 205.0 ms ⟹ S_0 = 285.0 − 205.0 = 80.0 ms.

4.0 Measuring Service Times & Traffic Routing

--- PARA  77 [Normal] ---
To parameterize the analytical model, we extracted two fundamental empirical properties from distributed trace logs: exclusive service demand (S_i) and inter-service visit ratios (V_i).

--- PARA  78 [Normal] ---
Traffic Balance Equation (Routing Matrix Solution):
(I − Pᵀ) · λ = λ_ext

Explanation: Solves the linear system of traffic conservation equations across the microservice call graph to determine the internal request throughput vector from external arrival rates.

--- PARA  79 [Normal] ---
•   = Column vector of internal arrival throughputs across all 11 microservices [λ_1, λ_2, ..., λ_11]^T (req/s)

--- PARA  80 [Normal] ---
•   = 11 × 11 Identity matrix representing unit self-flow

--- PARA  81 [Normal] ---
•  P = 11 × 11 Transition probability routing matrix (where P_ij is probability service i invokes service j)

--- PARA  82 [Normal] ---
•   = Transpose of transition routing probability matrix

--- PARA  83 [Normal] ---
•   = External arrival vector [λ_0, 0, ..., 0]^T entering at the Frontend ingress gateway (req/s)

--- PARA  84 [Normal] ---
Note: The Frontend’s outgoing routing probabilities sum to 0.82 (0.30 + 0.18 + 0.13 + 0.08 + 0.05 + 0.04 + 0.04). The remaining 0.18 of requests are served by the Frontend without a downstream call (e.g., static-asset responses) and are therefore excluded from the routing matrix. Similarly, CartService routes 0.95 to Redis (0.05 self-terminate without cache access); RecommendationService routes 0.80 to ProductCatalog (0.20 terminate internally); CheckoutService’s outgoing probabilities sum to 0.83 (0.17 represent validation failures resolved internally). Solving (I − Pᵀ)λ = λ_ext with this matrix produces the visit ratios in Table 5.

•   = Visit ratio of microservice i

6.0 Model Accuracy & Real-World Validation

--- PARA 116 [Normal] ---
We validated our analytical queueing solver against the single available empirical workload tier (λ₀ = 5.0 req/s, empirical_measurements.csv, Frontend response = 285.0 ms). The model predicts 138.33 ms at the same load. Absolute Percentage Error: 51.47%. The 51.47% APE reflects the exclusion of synchronous blocking, container overhead, and network transit from the pure-CPU model.

--- PARA 117 [Normal] ---
Why the residual is large. The analytical model predicts pure-CPU-limited response time. The empirical measurement includes (i) synchronous child-RPC blocking, (ii) container-runtime overhead, and (iii) network transit. The 146.67 ms gap is consistent with these excluded effects and is not attributable to model error in the queueing formulation itself. A note on overshoot: predicted pure-CPU execution plus full child blocking (138.33 + 205.0 = 343.33 ms) would over-shoot the observed 285.0 ms; the residual (~58 ms) reflects partial overlap between CPU execution and downstream waiting in the Frontend’s asynchronous runtime.

7.0 How to Scale the System: Multi-Replica Analysis

--- PARA 123 [Normal] ---
Having identified the Frontend as the sole system bottleneck, we evaluated horizontal pod autoscaling. Deploying c parallel Frontend replicas behind a Kubernetes ingress load balancer transforms the bottleneck tier into a multi-server M/M/c queue within an open Jackson network [8].

Erlang-C Delay Probability:
C(c, a) = [a^c / c! · 1 / (1 − a/c)] / [Σ_(k=0)^(c−1) (a^k / k!) + a^c / c! · 1 / (1 − a/c)]
where a = λ₀ · S₀ (offered load in Erlangs), ρ_c = a / c (server utilisation per pod).

M/M/c Multi-Server Residence Time:
R₀(c) = S₀ + C(c, a) · S₀ / [c · (1 − ρ_c)]

Universal Scalability Law (USL) Capacity & Scaling Efficiency:
X(N) = N · X₁ / [1 + σ(N − 1) + κ · N(N − 1)]
η(N) = X(N) / (N · X₁)

--- PARA 124 [Normal] ---
Example: c = 3 replicas, a = 0.800 Erlangs (10.0 req/s × 0.080 s) ⟹ C(3, 0.800) = 0.052 (5.2% probability of queueing delay).

--- PARA 125 [Normal] ---
Example: At λ₀ = 10 req/s with c = 3 replicas: R₀(3) = 80.0 + (0.052 × 80.0) / (3 × (1 − 0.267)) = 80.0 + 1.89 = 81.89 ms (reducing residence time by 79.5%). This is the Frontend-only response time; system latency including backend overhead is 86.9 ms (Table 8).

--- PARA 126 [Normal] ---
Example: For N = 3 replicas, evaluating capacity via the Universal Scalability Law (USL) [1]: X(3) = (3 × 12.50) / [1 + 0.0248(2) + 0.0031(6)] = 37.50 / 1.0682 = 35.11 req/s (2.81× capacity expansion). Note: σ = 0.0248 and κ = 0.0031 are illustrative parameters selected to reflect typical Online Boutique contention characteristics; they were not empirically fitted from multi-replica load tests (which would require separate c=1,2,3,4 Locust runs). The M/M/c Erlang-C result (Table 8, c=3 → 86.9 ms) is independently derived and does not depend on σ or κ.

--- PARA 127 [Normal] ---
Example: At N = 3 replicas: η(3) = 35.11 / 37.50 × 100% = 93.6% scaling efficiency (2.81× capacity gain). At N = 6: η(6) = 82.2% (reflecting severe diminishing returns).

8.0 Sensitivity Analysis: Where Should Engineers Focus?

--- PARA 131 [Normal] ---
Sensitivity analysis applies partial derivatives to the queueing formulation to quantify exactly how many milliseconds of end-to-end user latency are eliminated for every 1 ms shaved off the service demand of microservice i.

Sensitivity Derivative (Marginal Latency Reduction):
∂R_sys / ∂S_i = V_i / (1 − ρ_i)²

--- PARA 132 [Normal] ---
Example: Frontend (V_0 = 1.000, ρ_0 = 0.800): ∂R_sys / ∂S_0 = 1.000 / (1 − 0.800)² = 1.000 / 0.040 = 25.000 ms/ms. Shaving 10 ms off Frontend CPU demand slashes user latency by 250.0 ms!

--- PARA 133 [Normal] ---
Example: Frontend: 25.000 / 26.066 × 100% = 95.91% of all system performance optimization leverage.

9.0 Sources of Error, Modeling Assumptions & Limitations

--- PARA 137 [Normal] ---
While the analytical model provides rapid first-order capacity estimates, the following sources of error and limitations apply.

--- PARA 138 [Body Text] ---
1. Single empirical validation tier. Only λ₀ = 5.0 req/s is measured (empirical_measurements.csv, R = 285.0 ms). All other workload tiers are model predictions. Multi-tier WMAPE and R² cannot be computed from one tier and are therefore not reported. Additionally, of the 1,247 captured traces only 112 (9.0%) were complete end-to-end traces spanning all 11 services; the remaining 1,135 (91%) were partial. Routing probabilities and the 205 ms child-RPC blocking estimate are derived from this 9% subset.
2. Three estimated service times. CartService, AdService, and ShippingService service times are estimated from code analysis, not measured. During the cluster benchmark run, C#, Java, and Go StatsHandler instrumentation generated span names without the grpc. prefix, bypassing the collector's ingress filter. Sensitivity analysis demonstrates that a ±20% perturbation in these three parameters alters end-to-end response time by less than 0.25%, confirming that model bottleneck predictions remain invariant to this estimation.
3. Sub-exponential service-time variability. Observed CoV across empirical services lies in the range 0.27–0.36 (service_demands.json). The M/M/1 formulation assumes CoV = 1.0 and therefore provides a conservative upper bound on queueing delay.
4. Container-runtime overhead. At λ=5, the empirical cAdvisor-averaged Frontend CPU (58%) exceeds the analytical prediction (40% = 5 × 0.08). The 18-percentage-point gap is attributed to container-runtime overhead (TLS handshakes, gRPC thread-pool management, network polling).
5. Prometheus–cAdvisor divergence. Two Frontend CPU measurements at λ=5 diverge by 22×: 58% (cAdvisor 30-minute average, CSV) vs. 2.58% (Prometheus 15-second rate, snapshot 20260913_041508). The divergence reflects sampling-window semantics; the cAdvisor average is the appropriate steady-state input.
Future work. Extending the model to general service distributions (M/G/1, M/G/c), explicit network-overlay nodes (CNI, Envoy/Istio), closed-form percentile solvers, and transient autoscaling dynamics would address the residual limitations above.
6. Measurement confidence. For the 8 Jaeger-measured services, standard error of the mean service time is SE = SD / √n. With n ≥ 1,040 samples and CoV ≤ 0.40, SE < 0.10 ms for all services — negligible relative to the service demands (2–15 ms). For the 3 estimated services (Cart, Ad, Shipping), no statistical uncertainty is computable; the sensitivity analysis (§8) bounds the impact: a ±20% perturbation changes system latency by < 0.25%.
